## Project Overview

Iran’s nuclear program represents one of the most complex intersections of technical innovation, regional geopolitics, and international security law in modern history. Originally established with direct assistance from Western powers under the United States' Atoms for Peace program, the program transformed over several decades into a sophisticated, indigenous nuclear fuel cycle capable of uranium enrichment and heavy water research.

The primary objective of this repository is to track and document:
* The technological evolution of Iran's centrifuge technology (from early IR-1 designs to advanced IR-6 centrifuges).
* The legal trajectory of Iran’s commitments under the Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons (NPT).
* The strategic, diplomatic, and economic responses from international bodies such as the International Atomic Energy Agency (IAEA), the United Nations Security Council (UNSC), and regional alliances.

## Historical Eras

### Phase I: Atoms for Peace and Early Development (1957–1979)
The foundational infrastructure of Iran’s nuclear program was laid in March 1957 when the United States signed a civil nuclear cooperation agreement with the Iranian government under Shah Mohammad Reza Pahlavi. 

* **1967:** The Tehran Nuclear Research Center (TNRC) was established, anchored by a 5-megawatt thermal research reactor (TRR) supplied by the United States, utilizing highly enriched uranium (HEU) fuel.
* **1968:** Iran signed the Non-Proliferation Treaty (NPT), ratifying it in 1970.
* **1974:** The Atomic Energy Organization of Iran (AEOI) was created, setting an ambitious target to generate 23,000 megawatts of electricity via a network of commercial nuclear reactors built by Western vendors (such as Kraftwerk Union of West Germany and Framatome of France). Construction began on the Bushehr Nuclear Power Plant.

### Phase II: Post-Revolutionary Reconstruction (1979–2002)
Following the 1979 Islamic Revolution, international partnerships dissolved, and construction projects ground to a halt. Western technical assistance was withdrawn, and damaged infrastructure during the Iran-Iraq War further delayed nuclear ambitions.

* **1980s:** Iran shifted toward clandestine procurement networks to acquire enrichment technology, entering agreements with foreign suppliers and utilizing the A.Q. Khan network for centrifuge blueprints.
* **1995:** Iran signed a contract with Russia’s Ministry for Atomic Energy (Minatom) to complete Unit 1 of the Bushehr power plant using VVER-1000 pressurized water reactor technology.

### Phase III: The Clandestine Revelations and Sanctions Era (2002–2015)
In August 2002, the National Council of Resistance of Iran (NCRI) publicly revealed undisclosed nuclear facilities under construction at Natanz (uranium enrichment) and Arak (heavy water production).

* **2003:** Iran signed the IAEA Additional Protocol and temporarily suspended enrichment activity following negotiations with the EU-3 (France, Germany, and the UK).
* **2005–2006:** Suspension was lifted; enrichment resumed at Natanz. In response, the UN Security Council passed Resolution 1696, demanding the suspension of all enrichment and reprocessing activities.
* **2006–2012:** Unprecedented economic and financial sanctions were imposed by the UNSC, US, and EU. Simultaneously, undisclosed sites such as the Fordow Fuel Enrichment Plant (FFEP) were revealed in 2009.

### Phase IV: The JCPOA and Subsequent Escalations (2015–Present)
In July 2015, Iran and the P5+1 (US, UK, France, China, Russia, plus Germany) finalized the Joint Comprehensive Plan of Action (JCPOA). Iran agreed to severe limits on uranium enrichment, stockpile sizes, and centrifuge numbers in exchange for sanctions relief.

* **2018:** The United States unilaterally withdrew from the JCPOA, re-imposing maximum pressure sanctions.
* **2019–Present:** Iran incrementally exceeded JCPOA thresholds, expanding enrichment levels up to 20% and 60% $UF_6$, deploying advanced centrifuge cascades, and curtailing IAEA inspection access under modified monitoring protocols.

## Key Infrastructure and Strategic Facilities

* **Natanz (Fuel Enrichment Plant & Pilot Fuel Enrichment Plant):** Located in Isfahan Province, Natanz serves as the principal site for commercial-scale uranium enrichment, housing both underground production halls and surface-level research facilities.
* **Fordow (Fuel Enrichment Plant):** Built deep inside a mountain near Qom, Fordow was designed to withstand heavy air strikes and houses cascades utilized for high-grade enrichment operations.
* **Bushehr Nuclear Power Plant:** Iran's primary commercial nuclear power station, delivering electricity to the national grid under Russian fuel cycle supply agreements.
* **Arak (IR-40 Heavy Water Reactor):** A heavy water research reactor site designed for medical isotope production, historically scrutinized due to plutonium production potential, later re-designed under JCPOA terms.
* **Isfahan Nuclear Technology Center:** Key site housing conversion facilities ($UF_6$ production), fuel fabrication plants, and research laboratories.

## Regulatory and Safeguards Framework

Iran’s nuclear activities operate under an evolving matrix of legal agreements with the IAEA:
* **Comprehensive Safeguards Agreement (CSA):** Pursuant to Article III of the NPT, requiring material accounting for all source or special fissionable material.
* **Modified Code 3.1:** Subsidiary arrangement requiring notification to the IAEA as soon as a decision is made to construct new nuclear facilities.
* **Additional Protocol (INFCIRC/540):** Grants the IAEA broader complementary access to un-declared sites and locations.
